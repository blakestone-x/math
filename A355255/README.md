# Two conjectures on cyclic binary differences

Research notes for Blake Stone. Proof developed with OpenAI Codex.

Peter Kagey's A355255 starts with a binary necklace of $n$ beads. Replace each bit by its sum modulo 2 with the preceding bit, and repeat. Two necklaces are equal when one is a rotation of the other. Write $a(n,k)$ for the number of distinct necklaces visited from the $n$-bit encoding of $k$, including the starting necklace. The opposite choice of neighbor gives the same necklace at every step.

**Theorems.** For every $r\ge0$,

$$
a(2^r,1)=2^r+1,\qquad a(37\cdot2^r,1)=87{,}382\cdot2^r.
$$

The first identity proves Kagey's power-of-two-length conjecture. The second gives infinitely many counterexamples to his proposal that $a(n,1)$ is a power of two at every other length. The smallest counterexample is $n=37$.

## Doubling the length

Let $D$ denote the difference map on words and $N(w)$ the number of necklaces visited from $w$. Define $E(w)$ by writing a zero after every bit of $w$, and $B(w)$ by writing each bit twice. A direct calculation on adjacent bits gives

$$
D E(w)=B(w),\qquad D B(w)=E(Dw).
$$

The even iterates from $E(w)$ therefore encode the orbit of $w$ with $E$, and the odd iterates encode it with $B$. Each encoding is injective on rotation classes. For $E$, a nonzero word occupies only even positions, so any rotation between two such encodings is even. For $B$, taking the bits in the even positions of a rotated encoding recovers a rotation of its original word. The only class shared by the two images is the all-zero word, since a nonzero $B(w)$ occupies both parities. Hence

$$
N(E(w))=2N(w)-\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{the orbit of }w\text{ contains zero}\}}.
$$

Represent a word by the polynomial $w(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}w_i x^i$ modulo $x^n-1$ over $\mathbb F_2$. Rotation multiplies by $x$ and $D$ multiplies by $1+x$. If the singleton $w=1$ ever reaches zero, then it is zero at some time $2^j$. At that time its word is $(1+x)^{2^j}=1+x^{2^j}$, which vanishes exactly when $n\mid 2^j$. Conversely this divisibility suffices to reach zero. The singleton therefore reaches zero precisely at power-of-two lengths. It follows that

$$
a(2n,1)=
\begin{cases}
2a(n,1)-1,& n\text{ a power of two},\\
2a(n,1),&\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
$$

At length one the orbit is $1,0$. Induction proves $a(2^r,1)=2^r+1$. It also shows that a single counterexample at odd length generates counterexamples at all its power-of-two multiples.

## An exact certificate at length 37

Put $y=1+x$ and $H=1+x+\cdots+x^{36}$. The first step takes $1$ to $y$. Every later word has even parity, so the initial singleton cannot recur. For $p>0$, a return to the necklace of $y$ after $p$ further steps is equivalent to

$$
y^{p+1}\equiv x^h y\pmod{x^{37}-1}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y^p\equiv x^h\pmod H
$$

for some $h$. The equivalence follows from $x^{37}-1=yH$. Both $x$ and $y$ are units modulo $H$ because $H(0)=H(1)=1$ in $\mathbb F_2$. The least return time is therefore the order of $y$ in the finite group of units modulo the subgroup generated by $x$.

Take $P=87{,}381=3^2\cdot7\cdot19\cdot73$. Encode a polynomial by the integer whose bit $i$ is its coefficient of $x^i$. The following are exact residues of $y^{p+1}$ modulo $x^{37}-1$, written in hexadecimal.

| $p$ | Residue | Number of set bits |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $87{,}381$ | 0x3000 | 2 |
| $P/3=29{,}127$ | 0xe26323afd | 20 |
| $P/7=12{,}483$ | 0x1b4a5b07e0 | 18 |
| $P/19=4{,}599$ | 0x1434b0a0a0 | 12 |
| $P/73=1{,}197$ | 0x1a50a5f80f | 18 |

The first residue is $x^{12}y$. Each of the other four has more than two set bits and cannot be a rotation of $y$. The order of the class of $y$ divides $P$ and divides none of $P/3,P/7,P/19,P/73$. It is exactly $P$. Thus $a(37,1)=1+P=87{,}382$, strictly between $2^{16}$ and $2^{17}$. The doubling identity now proves the infinite family.

To reproduce every table entry, start at the mask 3 for $y$. For each set bit $j$ of $p$, XOR the current mask with its cyclic shift by $2^j\bmod37$. This multiplies by $1+x^{2^j}=y^{2^j}$. An independent direct traversal canonically rotates every word and first repeats the necklace at index 1 after visiting 87,382 distinct necklaces.

## Every starting word at a power-of-two length

When $n=2^r$, the polynomial ring is $\mathbb F_2[y]/(y^n)$. For $w\ne0$ let $v$ be the multiplicity of $y$ in $w$, and put $v=n$ for the zero word. A rotation preserves $v$ because $x=1+y$ is a unit. Each difference step increases $v$ by one until zero is reached. Consequently

$$
a(n,k)=n-v+1
$$

for every initial word at these lengths. Exactly one word gives count 1. For each $2\le d\le n+1$ there are $2^{d-2}$ labeled starting words with count $d$, since a polynomial of valuation $n+1-d$ has exactly $d-2$ free coefficients in the $y$ basis.

## Sources and verification

The two conjectures and the necklace convention are recorded in [OEIS A355255](https://oeis.org/A355255). For the earlier **labeled** Ducci period at length 37, Amos Ehrlich computed $3{,}233{,}097=37\cdot87{,}381$ in [Periods in Ducci's n-number game of differences](https://doi.org/10.1080/00150517.1990.12429467), Fibonacci Quarterly 28 (1990), 302–305. The table above independently certifies the period after identifying rotations.
